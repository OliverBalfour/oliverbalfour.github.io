A lot of people in the tech world are going through a period of angst right now. The job is changing, after all.

I quit a high-paying job at Jane Street a couple years ago without knowing what was next, so I thought I'd offer some of the realisations I came to in the hope it saves you some soul-searching.

Layoffs can cause a real psychic injury. "Companies don't care about you", goes the online refrain, from grizzled tech veterans.

While partially true, it betrays a cynicism that doesn't need to be there.

## What is a company, anyway?

It's a social construct. Try to build one yourself, or read honest accounts of how they're built (i.e. not official corporate lore and press releases), and you see how they are literally socially constructed from nothing.

A company is an idea in the heads of its employees. There's a brand, maybe some products, a group of people who know each other and show up to work every morning.

They're an aspirational ideal, not ultimately real.

## Aspirational ideal

When we anthropomorphise and mythologise companies, it can create real psychic injury. When we're laid off by email after giving up so much of our working lives, it can feel like betrayal.

The thing is, it was always an aspirational ideal. We were working towards creating this. That's the whole point, you're trying to make something happen in the world, hitting a moving target. Sometimes it falls short for reasons out of our control. Sometimes everyone gives up on the ideal and moves on.

But using the word "company", an abstraction, clouds what was really happening under the hood. Our coworkers often care about us, but in Big Tech executive leadership has so many staff they can only manage employees on a headcount basis.

Some decisions are made by our coworkers, some by HR, some by executive leadership, some by department heads, some by regulators. That's okay. Notice how the "company" didn't make any decisions and you'll save yourself a lot of hurt. "Companies don't care about you" is a parse error.

And some institutions are just meant to disintegrate. They had their time. I'm reminded of my time at university, jaded by feeling the university experience had been oversold. Lecturers were quitting, classes were poorly-taught. Nobody took responsibility for the systemic failures that surrounded them. Where strong leadership was present, leaders were scapegoated by the people causing the problems. I see parallels in the corporate and political worlds.

## Meaning

It still mattered, just not in the way I thought it did. I thought it was about the company and its mission, and if the company isn't what you thought it was, it feels like a betrayal. But it was always about the time I spent with interesting coworkers, and the way we helped our customers, the way I grew as a person. I just couldn't see it at the time.

Sometimes online I see kids with a bad home life use configuring Arch Linux as their escape from reality. I smile, because it reminds me of growing up. Did configuring Arch Linux matter? Not particularly. It might not matter in the way I thought it did then, but it still mattered. I found community and learned skills. I fit in. And so it angers me when I see kids who want to bond over learning say "i use arch btw" and get made fun of, and replies from people telling them to "RTFM". HN culture has, uh, room for improvement.

Did learning the quadratic equation in high school "matter"? I look back fondly on my high school math classes. They taught me I had talents I couldn't see in myself, they made me feel at home with my peers, and yes, they taught me some useful algebra. When people ask whether learning trigonometry "matters", they're often asking the wrong question. (If the class is toxic, the teacher is toxic, and the way it's taught injures your self-esteem, then yes, it wasn't helpful.)

Every time you go through a new stage of life, you look at your past through new eyes. Working in corporate wasn't much different from configuring Arch Linux or learning math as a kid. The work itself often isn't what matters. It was still worth it. I do wish tech workers did more for the world, though.

## Change

There is a lot of vitriol directed towards tech workers. Much of it is earned. People who worked at Facebook, for instance, have a lot of soul-searching to do. Not everyone will be compassionate, and that's okay. People you've hurt don't owe you that grace. You can still find your people.

The best programmers I know have stopped writing code by hand. "It takes the joy out of my job," they say. I thought I'd escape by working on startups, but having seen it up close, the startup world seems corrupt to me now. I plan to retrain and do drug discovery.

I don't have any idea what the industry will look like. Nobody does. Maybe a lot of tech jobs start to look more like technician jobs than engineering jobs. You can't reason from first principles about this, though. Many of the factors depend on the mindsets of decisionmakers, where perception matters more than reality. But models will get cheaper and better, as will their harnesses. Anything digitised enough for LLMs to operate on will change, from cybersecurity to programming.

What's important is that we support each other. And that we're honest with ourselves and each other. Life might look different to how we thought it would, and that's okay.
